Topic 1 — Present Perfect
Slide 2 Present Perfect — Overview
Point to the three boxes on the slide as you mention each use.

The first topic is the Present Perfect.

The Present Perfect is a verb tense that we use to connect the past with the present. It is different from the Past Simple because the action is still relevant NOW — it has a connection to the present moment.

We use the Present Perfect in three main situations.

The first one is for life experiences — things you have or have not done at some point in your life, without saying exactly when. For example: "I have visited Paris."

The second one is for recent events — actions that just happened and still affect the present. For example: "She has broken her arm." — the arm is still broken right now.

And the third one is for unfinished time — situations that started in the past and are still continuing. For example: "I have lived here for five years." — and I still live here today.

So remember: if the action has a connection to the present, we use the Present Perfect.

Slide 3 Present Perfect — Auxiliary: HAVE / HAS
Read the two columns clearly. Pause between HAVE and HAS to give the audience time to absorb the rule.

Now, let's look at the auxiliary verb for the Present Perfect. The auxiliary is HAVE or HAS.

Choosing between them is simple. We use HAVE with the subjects: I, You, We, and They.

We use HAS with the subjects: He, She, and It — that is, the third person singular.

In negative sentences, we use: have not, which contracts to haven't, and has not, which contracts to hasn't.

For example: "She hasn't called me." — "We haven't seen that film."

In questions, we place HAVE or HAS at the beginning of the sentence, before the subject.

For example: "Have you finished your homework?" — "Has she arrived yet?"

So: HAVE goes with I, you, we, they — and HAS goes with he, she, it. This is the rule you must always remember.

Slide 4 Present Perfect — Structure & Formation (all three forms)
The slide is titled "Past Simple · Structure & Formation" but it actually shows Present Perfect structures. Read it as Present Perfect — that is the correct topic.

Now let's look at how to build the Present Perfect in all three sentence forms.

For affirmative sentences, the structure is:

Subject + have / has + Past Participle (V3)

The Past Participle — also called V3 — is the third form of the verb. For regular verbs, it is the same as the past form: walked, visited, finished. For irregular verbs, you must learn them: seen, eaten, gone, written, bought.

Examples: "She has visited France." — "They have finished their homework."

For negative sentences, the structure is:

Subject + have not / has not + Past Participle

Examples: "He hasn't called me." — "We haven't seen that film."

For questions, the structure is:

Have / Has + Subject + Past Participle ?

Examples: "Have you ever eaten sushi?" — "Has she arrived yet?"

So remember the three structures: affirmative uses have or has + V3. Negative adds not. And questions invert have or has with the subject.

Slide 5 Present Perfect — Key Signal Words (Time Expressions)
Point to each card on the slide as you explain it. Take your time — these 7 words are very important for students.

One of the most helpful things about the Present Perfect is that it has specific signal words — time expressions that tell you when to use it.

Let me go through each one.

EVER — we use this in questions to ask about life experience at any point in time. Example: "Have you ever been to Paris?" — meaning: at any moment in your life, did you go to Paris?

NEVER — this means not at any time in your life. Example: "She has never eaten sushi." — in her entire life, she has not eaten it.

ALREADY — this goes in affirmative sentences and means the action happened sooner than expected. Example: "I have already finished." — I finished before you expected me to.

YET — this is used in negatives and questions and means "up to this moment." Example: "Has he called yet?" — meaning: has he called up to now?

JUST — this means very recently, a moment ago. Example: "They have just left." — they left only minutes ago.

FOR — this expresses a duration of time. Example: "She has lived here for five years." — the total amount of time is five years.

And SINCE — this gives the starting point of an action. Example: "He has worked here since 2019." — he started in 2019 and is still working here now.

The key difference between FOR and SINCE: FOR is followed by a period of time — for five years, for two months. SINCE is followed by a point in time — since Monday, since 2019, since I was a child.

Topic 2 — Present Perfect vs Past Simple
Slide 7 Present Perfect VS Past Simple — Section Intro
This is the "VS" slide — your voice should show contrast. Use a clear pause between the two sides.

Now let's move to our second topic: Present Perfect versus Past Simple.

These two tenses both talk about the past — but they are very different in meaning and in use. This is one of the most common points of confusion for English learners, so let's make it very clear.

Look at these two sentences on the screen:

On the left: "I have seen that film." — Present Perfect.

On the right: "I saw that film last night." — Past Simple.

Both sentences are correct, but they communicate different things. The first one — Present Perfect — tells us that seeing the film is part of my experience, connected to now. The second one — Past Simple — tells us exactly when it happened: last night. It is finished and in the past.

The key question to ask yourself is: Do I know when it happened? If yes — use Past Simple. If the time is not important or not mentioned — use Present Perfect.

Slide 8 Present Perfect vs Past Simple — Key Differences
Point to each row of the table on screen as you explain it. Read the left column first, then the right column for each comparison.

Let's look at the key differences between these two tenses, side by side.

First difference — time: the Present Perfect does NOT mention a specific time. The Past Simple DOES mention or imply a specific time.

Second difference — connection to the present: the Present Perfect is connected to the present moment. The Past Simple describes a finished action with no connection to now.

Third difference — signal words: the Present Perfect uses words like already, yet, ever, just, for, and since. The Past Simple uses words like yesterday, last year, ago, and in 2020.

Now look at the examples in the table:

Present Perfect: "I have lost my keys." — this means I lost them and they are still lost. There is a present consequence.

Past Simple: "I lost my keys yesterday." — I know exactly when it happened. The focus is on the past event.

Present Perfect: "She has never tried sushi." — this is her life experience up to now.

Past Simple: "She didn't try sushi last night." — a specific moment in the past, finished.

So the main rule is: no specific time = Present Perfect. Specific time = Past Simple.

Slide 9 Present Perfect vs Past Simple — Auxiliary Comparison
This is a detailed slide with two text boxes. Read the left box first (Present Perfect), then the right box (Past Simple). Speak slowly — there is a lot of important information here.

Now let's compare the auxiliary verbs used in each tense.

For the Present Perfect, the auxiliary verbs are HAVE and HAS. These auxiliaries are always present — in affirmative sentences, in negatives, and in questions. You cannot remove them.

HAS is used with the third person singular — he, she, it. HAVE is used with all other subjects — I, you, we, they. These auxiliaries are always followed by the verb in its past participle form — also called V3.

In informal speech, they are often contracted: "I've finished", "she's arrived", "we've studied." And in negatives: haven't and hasn't.

For the Past Simple, the auxiliary is DID. But here is an important difference: DID is only used in negative and interrogative sentences — not in affirmative ones.

In affirmative sentences, Past Simple does not use an auxiliary at all — the main verb changes directly to its past form: "She went to school." — "They bought a car."

But when you form a negative with DIDN'T or a question with DID, the main verb must return to its base form — the infinitive. Never say "He didn't went" — the correct form is "He didn't go."

So the key contrast is: Present Perfect always needs HAVE or HAS in every sentence form. Past Simple only uses DID in negatives and questions.

These pairs show perfectly how the two tenses work and why choosing the right one changes the meaning of your sentence.

Topic 3 — Indefinite Pronouns
Slide 11 Indefinite Pronouns — Overview
New topic! Show energy. Point to the three colored boxes: THINGS, PEOPLE, PLACES.

Now we move to our third and final topic: Indefinite Pronouns.

Indefinite pronouns are words that refer to people, things, or places without being specific. Instead of saying "a specific person" or "a specific thing," we use these pronouns when we do not know — or do not want to specify — exactly who, what, or where.

In English, indefinite pronouns are formed using four prefixes: SOME-, ANY-, NO-, and EVERY-. Each prefix combines with three endings: -thing, -body / -one, and -where.

This gives us three categories:

For THINGS: something, anything, nothing, everything.

For PEOPLE: somebody, anybody, nobody, everybody.

For PLACES: somewhere, anywhere, nowhere, everywhere.

Each of these twelve words has a specific rule for when to use it — and that is exactly what we are going to learn right now.

Slide 12 Indefinite Pronouns — Things: something · anything · nothing · everything
Point to each of the four cards as you explain. Read the example sentence clearly with intonation.

Let's start with the group for THINGS.

SOMETHING — we use this in affirmative sentences to refer to an unspecified thing. Example: "I want something to eat." — I want a thing to eat, but I'm not saying exactly what.

ANYTHING — we use this in questions and negative sentences. Example: "Do you need anything?" — in a question. And: "I don't need anything." — in a negative.

NOTHING — this has a negative meaning by itself. It means zero things. Example: "There is nothing in the fridge." — the fridge is completely empty. Be careful: because NOTHING already has a negative meaning, you should NEVER use it with "not" — that would be a double negative, which is incorrect in English.

EVERYTHING — this refers to all things, the complete set. Example: "She knows everything about grammar." — all things, without exception.

So to summarize for things: SOMETHING in affirmatives, ANYTHING in questions and negatives, NOTHING when you mean zero things, and EVERYTHING when you mean all things.

Slide 13 Indefinite Pronouns — People: somebody · anybody · nobody · everybody
The structure is identical to THINGS — same rules, just applied to people. You can remind the audience of the pattern.

Now let's look at the same group of words, but for PEOPLE. The rules are exactly the same — only the ending changes: instead of -thing, we use -body.

SOMEBODY — used in affirmative sentences. It refers to an unspecified person. Example: "Somebody called you this morning." — a person called, but I don't know who.

ANYBODY — used in questions and negatives. Example: "Is anybody home?" — in a question. And: "I didn't see anybody." — in a negative sentence.

NOBODY — means no person at all. It has negative meaning, just like NOTHING. Example: "Nobody knows the answer." — not a single person knows. Again, never combine NOBODY with "not" — it creates a double negative.

EVERYBODY — refers to all people, every single person. Example: "Everybody passed the English exam!" — all students, without exception.

And just like with THINGS: SOMEBODY in affirmatives, ANYBODY in questions and negatives, NOBODY for no person, EVERYBODY for all people.

Slide 14 Indefinite Pronouns — Places: somewhere · anywhere · nowhere · everywhere
Third and final group. Point to each card. Students often mix up NOWHERE and ANYWHERE — clarify clearly.

The third group is for PLACES. Now the ending is -where. And the rules — are exactly the same.

SOMEWHERE — used in affirmative sentences to refer to an unspecified place. Example: "Let's go somewhere quiet to study." — we want a place, but we haven't decided exactly where.

ANYWHERE — used in questions and negatives. Example: "Did you go anywhere last weekend?" — in a question. And: "I didn't go anywhere." — in a negative. This means I stayed in one place all weekend.

NOWHERE — means no place at all. It already has negative meaning. Example: "There is nowhere to sit in this room." — there is not a single free seat. Remember: do not add "not" with NOWHERE.

EVERYWHERE — means in all places, every location. Example: "I have looked everywhere for my keys." — I searched in every possible place.

So: SOMEWHERE — affirmative. ANYWHERE — questions and negatives. NOWHERE — no place, already negative. EVERYWHERE — all places, total.

Slide 15 Indefinite Pronouns — Usage Rules: Some- vs Any- vs No- vs Every-
This slide has a summary table. Read it row by row. Emphasize the IMPORTANT TIP at the bottom — it is a common mistake.

Now let's look at the complete usage rules. This table on the screen summarizes when to use each prefix.

SOME- — words with SOME- go in affirmative sentences: yes. In questions, SOME- can be used in offers and requests — for example, "Would you like something to drink?" — but generally no in regular questions. And in negatives: no.

ANY- — words with ANY- do not go in affirmative sentences. But they work perfectly in questions and in negatives. This is why we say "I don't need anything" and NOT "I don't need something."

NO- — words with NO- can appear in affirmative sentences, but they already carry the negative meaning. Example: "Nobody called." — this is grammatically affirmative but means there were no calls. In questions they are rare, and in negatives you must never use them because that creates a double negative.

EVERY- — words with EVERY- can be used in affirmative sentences, questions, and negatives. Example: "Everybody is here." — "Has everybody finished?" — "Not everybody agreed."

And the most important tip — please pay attention to this: NOBODY, NOTHING, and NOWHERE already have negative meaning. Never, ever combine them with "not."

For example: Do NOT say "There is nowhere not to go." — just say "There is nowhere to go." Do NOT say "Nobody didn't come." — just say "Nobody came." This mistake is very common, so please remember it.

Diapositiva 2 Presente Perfecto — Visión General
Señala las tres cajas en la diapositiva a medida que mencionas cada uso.

El primer tema es el Presente Perfecto.

El Presente Perfecto es un tiempo verbal que usamos para conectar el pasado con el presente. Es diferente del Pasado Simple porque la acción todavía es relevante AHORA — tiene una conexión con el momento presente.

Usamos el Presente Perfecto en tres situaciones principales.

La primera es para experiencias de vida — cosas que has hecho o no has hecho en algún momento de tu vida, sin decir exactamente cuándo. Por ejemplo: "I have visited Paris" (He visitado París).

La segunda es para eventos recientes — acciones que acaban de ocurrir y todavía afectan el presente. Por ejemplo: "She has broken her arm" (Se ha roto el brazo) — el brazo todavía está roto en este momento.

Y la tercera es para tiempo inacabado — situaciones que comenzaron en el pasado y todavía continúan. Por ejemplo: "I have lived here for five years" (He vivido aquí por cinco años) — y todavía vivo aquí hoy.

Así que recuerda: si la acción tiene una conexión con el presente, usamos el Presente Perfecto.

Diapositiva 3 Presente Perfecto — Auxiliar: HAVE / HAS
Lee las dos columnas claramente. Haz una pausa entre HAVE y HAS para darle tiempo a la audiencia de asimilar la regla.

Ahora, veamos el verbo auxiliar para el Presente Perfecto. El auxiliar es HAVE o HAS.

Elegir entre ellos es simple. Usamos HAVE con los sujetos: I, You, We y They.

Usamos HAS con los sujetos: He, She y It — es decir, la tercera persona del singular.

En oraciones negativas, usamos: have not, que se contrae a haven't, y has not, que se contrae a hasn't.

Por ejemplo: "She hasn't called me." — "We haven't seen that film."

En preguntas, colocamos HAVE o HAS al principio de la oración, antes del sujeto.

Por ejemplo: "Have you finished your homework?" — "Has she arrived yet?"

Entonces: HAVE va con I, you, we, they — y HAS va con he, she, it. Esta es la regla que siempre debes recordar.

Diapositiva 4 Presente Perfecto — Estructura y Formación
La diapositiva se titula "Past Simple · Structure & Formation" pero en realidad muestra estructuras de Presente Perfecto. Léelo como Presente Perfecto — ese es el tema correcto.

Ahora veamos cómo construir el Presente Perfecto en las tres formas de oración.

Para oraciones afirmativas, la estructura es:

Sujeto + have / has + Pasado Participio (V3)

El Pasado Participio — también llamado V3 — es la tercera forma del verbo. Para los verbos regulares, es igual que la forma pasada: walked, visited, finished. Para los verbos irregulares, debes aprenderlos: seen, eaten, gone, written, bought.

Ejemplos: "She has visited France." — "They have finished their homework."

Para oraciones negativas, la estructura es:

Sujeto + have not / has not + Pasado Participio

Ejemplos: "He hasn't called me." — "We haven't seen that film."

Para preguntas, la estructura es:

Have / Has + Sujeto + Pasado Participio ?

Ejemplos: "Have you ever eaten sushi?" — "Has she arrived yet?"

Así que recuerda las tres estructuras: la afirmativa usa have o has + V3. La negativa añade not. Y en las preguntas se invierte have o has con el sujeto.

Una de las cosas más útiles del Presente Perfecto es que tiene palabras de señalización específicas — expresiones de tiempo que te indican cuándo usarlo.

Permítanme repasar cada una.

Ahora pasemos a nuestro segundo tema: Presente Perfecto versus Pasado Simple.

Estos dos tiempos hablan sobre el pasado — pero son muy diferentes en significado y uso. Este es uno de los puntos más comunes de confusión para los estudiantes de inglés, así que vamos a dejarlo muy claro.

Miren estas dos oraciones en la pantalla:

A la izquierda: "I have seen that film." — Presente Perfecto.

A la derecha: "I saw that film last night." — Pasado Simple.

Ambas oraciones son correctas, pero comunican cosas diferentes. La primera — Presente Perfecto — nos dice que ver la película es parte de mi experiencia, conectada con el ahora. La segunda — Pasado Simple — nos dice exactamente cuándo sucedió: anoche. Está terminada y en el pasado.

La pregunta clave que debes hacerte es: ¿Sé cuándo ocurrió? Si la respuesta es sí — usa el Pasado Simple. Si el tiempo no es importante o no se menciona — usa el Presente Perfecto.

Diapositiva 9 Presente Perfecto vs Pasado Simple — Comparación de Auxiliares
Esta es una diapositiva detallada con dos cuadros de texto. Lee primero el cuadro izquierdo, luego el derecho. Habla despacio — hay mucha información importante aquí.

Ahora comparemos los verbos auxiliares que se usan en cada tiempo.

Para el Presente Perfecto, los verbos auxiliares son HAVE y HAS. Estos auxiliares siempre están presentes — en oraciones afirmativas, en negativas y en preguntas. No puedes quitarlos.

HAS se usa con la tercera persona del singular — he, she, it. HAVE se usa con todos los demás sujetos — I, you, we, they. Estos auxiliares siempre van seguidos por el verbo en su forma de participio pasado — también llamado V3.

En el lenguaje informal, a menudo se contraen: "I've finished", "she's arrived", "we've studied." Y en negativas: haven't y hasn't.

Para el Pasado Simple, el auxiliar es DID. Pero aquí hay una diferencia importante: DID solo se usa en oraciones negativas e interrogativas — no en las afirmativas.

En las oraciones afirmativas, el Pasado Simple no usa un auxiliar en absoluto — el verbo principal cambia directamente a su forma pasada: "She went to school." — "They bought a car."

Pero cuando formas una negativa con DIDN'T o una pregunta con DID, el verbo principal debe volver a su forma base — el infinitivo. Nunca digas "He didn't went" — la forma correcta es "He didn't go."

Así que el contraste clave es: el Presente Perfecto siempre necesita HAVE o HAS en cada forma de oración. El Pasado Simple solo usa DID en negativas y preguntas.

Diapositiva 10 Presente Perfecto vs Pasado Simple — Oraciones Lado a Lado
Lee cada par de oraciones. Señala a la izquierda, luego a la derecha. Haz que el contraste sea claro con tu voz — el Presente Perfecto suena "abierto", el Pasado Simple suena "cerrado y terminado".

Comparemos ahora pares de oraciones directamente. Estos son ejemplos reales que muestran cómo la misma idea cambia de significado dependiendo del tiempo verbal.

Primer par:

Presente Perfecto: "I have visited Colombia three times." — esta es mi experiencia de vida. No digo cuándo. Podría ser en cualquier momento del pasado.

Pasado Simple: "I visited Colombia in 2019." — se menciona un año específico. La acción está terminada y situada en el pasado.

Segundo par:

Presente Perfecto: "Have you eaten yet?" — la pregunta se refiere a cualquier momento hasta ahora, el día de hoy.

Pasado Simple: "Did you eat dinner last night?" — se menciona un momento específico: anoche.

Tercer par:

Presente Perfecto: "She has just arrived." — ella llegó hace unos momentos. La palabra "just" nos dice que es muy reciente y está conectada con el ahora.

Pasado Simple: "She arrived at 8 pm." — se da la hora exacta, por lo que es Pasado Simple.

Cuarto par:

Presente Perfecto: "We have studied English for two years." — empezamos hace dos años y TODAVÍA estamos estudiando ahora.

Pasado Simple: "We studied English last semester." — fue un período específico que ya terminó.

Estos pares muestran perfectamente cómo funcionan los dos tiempos y por qué elegir el correcto cambia el significado de tu oración.

Tema 3 — Pronombres Indefinidos
Diapositiva 11 Pronombres Indefinidos — Visión General
¡Nuevo tema! Muestra energía. Señala las tres cajas de colores: COSAS, PERSONAS, LUGARES.

Ahora pasamos a nuestro tercer y último tema: Pronombres Indefinidos.

Los pronombres indefinidos son palabras que se refieren a personas, cosas o lugares sin ser específicos. En lugar de decir "una persona específica" o "una cosa específica", usamos estos pronombres cuando no sabemos — o no queremos especificar — exactamente quién, qué o dónde.

En inglés, los pronombres indefinidos se forman usando cuatro prefijos: SOME-, ANY-, NO-, y EVERY-. Cada prefijo se combina con tres terminaciones: -thing, -body / -one, y -where.

Esto nos da tres categorías:

Para COSAS: something, anything, nothing, everything.

Para PERSONAS: somebody, anybody, nobody, everybody.

Para LUGARES: somewhere, anywhere, nowhere, everywhere.

Cada una de estas doce palabras tiene una regla específica para cuando usarla — y eso es exactamente lo que vamos a aprender ahora mismo.

Diapositiva 12 Pronombres Indefinidos — Cosas: something · anything · nothing · everything
Señala cada una de las cuatro tarjetas mientras explicas. Lee la oración de ejemplo claramente con entonación.

Comencemos con el grupo para COSAS (Things).

SOMETHING — lo usamos en oraciones afirmativas para referirnos a una cosa no especificada. Ejemplo: "I want something to eat." — Quiero una cosa para comer, pero no digo exactamente qué.

ANYTHING — lo usamos en preguntas y oraciones negativas. Ejemplo: "¿Do you need anything?" — en una pregunta. Y: "I don't need anything." — en una negativa.

NOTHING — esto tiene un significado negativo por sí mismo. Significa cero cosas (nada). Ejemplo: "There is nothing in the fridge." — el refrigerador está completamente vacío. Ten cuidado: porque NOTHING ya tiene un significado negativo, NUNCA debes usarlo con la palabra "not" — eso sería una doble negación, lo cual es incorrecto en inglés.

EVERYTHING — esto se refiere a todas las cosas, el conjunto completo (todo). Ejemplo: "She knows everything about grammar." — todas las cosas, sin excepción.

Así que para resumir para cosas: SOMETHING en afirmativas, ANYTHING en preguntas y negativas, NOTHING cuando te refieres a cero cosas, y EVERYTHING cuando te refieres a todas las cosas.

Diapositiva 13 Pronombres Indefinidos — Personas: somebody · anybody · nobody · everybody
La estructura es idéntica a COSAS — mismas reglas, solo aplicadas a personas. Puedes recordarle a la audiencia el patrón.

Ahora veamos el mismo grupo de palabras, pero para PERSONAS (People). Las reglas son exactamente las mismas — solo cambia la terminación: en lugar de -thing, usamos -body.

SOMEBODY — se usa en oraciones afirmativas. Se refiere a una persona no especificada (alguien). Ejemplo: "Somebody called you this morning." — una persona llamó, pero no sé quién.

ANYBODY — se usa en preguntas y negativas. Ejemplo: "¿Is anybody home?" — en una pregunta. Y: "I didn't see anybody." — en una oración negativa.

NOBODY — significa ninguna persona en absoluto (nadie). Tiene un significado negativo, al igual que NOTHING. Ejemplo: "Nobody knows the answer." — ni una sola persona lo sabe. Nuevamente, nunca combines NOBODY con "not" — crea una doble negación.

EVERYBODY — se refiere a todas las personas, cada individuo (todos). Ejemplo: "¡Everybody passed the English exam!" — todos los estudiantes, sin excepción.

Y al igual que con las COSAS: SOMEBODY en afirmativas, ANYBODY en preguntas y negativas, NOBODY para ninguna persona, EVERYBODY para todas las personas.

Diapositiva 14 Pronombres Indefinidos — Lugares: somewhere · anywhere · nowhere · everywhere
Tercer y último grupo. Señala cada tarjeta. Los estudiantes a menudo confunden NOWHERE y ANYWHERE — acláralo bien.

El tercer grupo es para LUGARES (Places). Ahora la terminación es -where. Y las reglas — son exactamente las mismas.

SOMEWHERE — se usa en oraciones afirmativas para referirse a un lugar no especificado (algún lugar). Ejemplo: "Let's go somewhere quiet to study." — queremos un lugar, pero no hemos decidido exactamente dónde.

ANYWHERE — se usa en preguntas y negativas. Ejemplo: "¿Did you go anywhere last weekend?" — en una pregunta. Y: "I didn't go anywhere." — en una negativa. Esto significa que me quedé en un solo lugar todo el fin de semana.

NOWHERE — significa en ningún lugar. Ya tiene un significado negativo. Ejemplo: "There is nowhere to sit in this room." — no hay ni un solo asiento libre. Recuerda: no agregues "not" con NOWHERE.

EVERYWHERE — significa en todos los lugares, en cada ubicación. Ejemplo: "I have looked everywhere for my keys." — busqué en todos los lugares posibles.

Entonces: SOMEWHERE — afirmativa. ANYWHERE — preguntas y negativas. NOWHERE — ningún lugar, ya es negativo. EVERYWHERE — todos los lugares, total.

Diapositiva 15 Pronombres Indefinidos — Reglas de Uso: Some- vs Any- vs No- vs Every-
Esta diapositiva tiene una tabla resumen. Léela fila por fila. Enfatiza el CONSEJO IMPORTANTE en la parte inferior — es un error común.

Ahora veamos las reglas de uso completas. Esta tabla en la pantalla resume cuándo usar cada prefijo.

SOME- — las palabras con SOME- van en oraciones afirmativas: sí. En preguntas, SOME- se puede usar en ofertas y solicitudes — por ejemplo, "¿Would you like something to drink?" — pero generalmente no en preguntas regulares. Y en negativas: no.

ANY- — las palabras con ANY- no van en oraciones afirmativas. Pero funcionan perfectamente en preguntas y en negativas. Por eso decimos "I don't need anything" y NO "I don't need something."

NO- — las palabras con NO- pueden aparecer en oraciones afirmativas, pero ya llevan el significado negativo. Ejemplo: "Nobody called." — esto es gramaticalmente afirmativo pero significa que no hubo llamadas. En preguntas son raras, y en negativas nunca debes usarlas porque eso crea una doble negación.

EVERY- — las palabras con EVERY- se pueden usar en oraciones afirmativas, preguntas y negativas. Ejemplo: "Everybody is here." — "¿Has everybody finished?" — "Not everybody agreed."

Y el consejo más importante — por favor, presten atención a esto: NOBODY, NOTHING y NOWHERE ya tienen un significado negativo. Nunca, jamás los combinen con la palabra "not."

Por ejemplo: NO digan "There is nowhere not to go." — solo digan "There is nowhere to go." NO digan "Nobody didn't come." — solo digan "Nobody came." Este error es muy común, así que por favor recuérdenlo.
